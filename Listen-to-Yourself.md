# Listen to Yourself: The Grammar of the Trick

The walls of the Trick are built by those who perform it. Most of that performance is not done with the hands. It is spoken.

A cast does not improvise. It has lines, and the lines are written in a grammar so familiar we no longer hear it. A small word that turns a choice into a cause. A pronoun that hands the responsibility to somebody else. A verb that makes an agreement sound like a law of nature. These are not lies. They are artifices: made things, crafted to work without anyone noticing them.

Three words can catch them, depending on how you say them. Listen to yourself.

## I. …listen to yourself

The first reading is silent. It is the thought you have while someone else is talking.

A minister says "mistakes were made". The mistakes are there; the people who made them have vanished from the sentence. A presenter says "the market wants reassurance", and a pattern drawn from millions of separate decisions becomes a creature with moods. A neighbour shakes their head and says "surely someone must do something about it". Read it slowly. *Surely*: certainty, offered precisely where none exists. *Someone*: anyone but the speaker. *Must*: an obligation with no one doing the obliging. *Do something*: an action with no shape. *About it*: around a problem left unnamed. Every slot in the sentence is a placeholder. It sounds like a call to action and guarantees there will be none.

And you think, quietly: listen to yourself.

This reading is easy, and it is a pleasure. The Trick is so much clearer in other people's mouths.

## II. Listen to yourself!

Now say it out loud, and point it the other way.

The silent thought about the neighbour is itself a line. It sorts the world into people who are fooled and one person who is not. That is the generic "they" again, wearing better clothes. The same artifices are in your mouth. They are in everyone's.

"I'd love to, but I have to work." "People have rent to pay." "That's just how it is." "Everyone does it." "I can't, because…"

Take the most common of them slowly. *They have rent to pay.* *They*: always someone else, a face in the clouds. *Have*: you have hands; you do not have rent in that sense. You hold an agreement, and the verb hides it. *Rent*: payment for permission to stay on ground you are already standing on. *To pay*: in tokens, earned by performing the very thing the sentence is excusing. The reason for performing is the performance. And the hinge holding it all together is *because*, the smallest word in the script. It turns a choice into a cause and makes the second half of a sentence sound like it explains the first, when it only excuses it.

No one is exempt, including the page. The essay before this one, written to expose the Trick, uses "because" four times. One of them describes people handing over money "because everyone else does". It says "the walls are real", and the little verb "are" fixes in place something that is only ever performed. Even a text that sees the Trick has to speak its language.

## III. Listen to yourself

The third reading is plain. No silence, no exclamation mark. Just the practice.

It starts with going slowly. Take a sentence you said today, or one you are about to say, and read it word by word, as if each word had to justify itself. Most sentences survive. Some do not.

The ones that do not tend to use the same few artifices:

- **because**: a choice dressed as a cause.
- **they, everyone, people**: the crowd as subject, so that no one in particular is.
- **have to, must, need to**: an agreement spoken as if it were gravity.
- **the economy, the market, the system**: a process turned into a thing, so it can want and demand.
- **mistakes were made, it was decided**: the action kept, the actor removed.
- **is**: that's just how it is. The performed, fixed in place.

Then try the substitution test. Replace every placeholder with something specific. "Someone must do something about it" becomes "I will do this, about that, by Friday." Either the sentence becomes a commitment, or you hear that you never meant one. Swap "have to" for "choose to" and see if it is still true. Swap "they" for "I". Swap "because" for "and" and see whether the second half still explains the first.

One warning, and it matters. Once you start listening, the artifices are everywhere, and the temptation is to hear a trick in every sentence. That is pattern-seeking too, another face in the clouds. Not every "because" is an excuse. Sometimes the rain really is why the ground is wet. The practice is not suspicion. It is attention.

## Say it slowly

The Trick does not need you to believe it. It needs you to keep saying your lines. And the lines are not handed to you by anyone. You learned them the way you learned to speak, by hearing them everywhere, and you repeat them because… there it is again.

You will not stop speaking the grammar. No one can; it is the language we have. But a line you can hear is a line you can choose whether to say. That is all recognition ever was: not escape, not a better script, just the moment you hear your own voice reciting.

So the next time you catch yourself explaining why you can't, why someone should, why that is just how it is, stop halfway. Say it again, slowly, one word at a time.

Nothing stands.
