# Nothing. Really. Matters.

## I. Necessary Fiction

Belief is the operating system of the modern world.

If we look past the official language of civic duty and economic stewardship, the entire societal structure can seem like a grand illusion. The government creates a fiat currency out of thin air, forces its use by demanding it back as taxes, and pays its own employees with the very tokens it fabricates. It operates neither by market pricing nor by open competition, but through an exclusive mandate over legal and civic frameworks. From a purely logical standpoint, the setup looks like a closed-loop accounting trick, a giant confidence game designed to maintain psychological and physical control over a population.

Yet this system functions day after day because it is a necessary fiction.

A necessary fiction is something factually made up, but socially required. Money, borders, corporations and legal authority do not exist in nature; they are artificial constructs with no intrinsic value. They are stories we invented. A human being can only naturally trust a small tribe of people, yet millions of strangers can peacefully inhabit the same cities and trade across global networks by sharing these myths.

When everyone shares the fiction, the illusion behaves exactly like reality. A worker hands a piece of plastic to a stranger who speaks a different language, and the stranger hands over food, clothes, or the keys to a vehicle. The stranger does not trust the individual; they trust the myth of money.

Because the system is built on a shared construct, power does not reside in government buildings or the ink on paper bills. It resides in the minds of the people who choose to play along. If a population collectively stops believing, the fiction evaporates. The farms, the factories, the labour remain unchanged, but the economy grinds to an immediate halt.

We live in a civilisation built upon an engineered illusion, where the only thing required to keep the machine from falling into chaos is for everyone to keep believing the lie.

That is the explanation. It is tidy, it is widely held, and almost every sentence of it is believable.

## II. They never stopped believing

Take the place where a fiction most visibly died. When the Zimbabwe dollar collapsed, the people holding it did not stop believing in money. It might have helped if they had. They believed in money the whole way down. They simply moved their belief to the US dollar, to the rand, to whatever still held.

So belief is not the switch the explanation said it was. The fiction died while it was still believed. And nothing halted. People traded in other currencies, in credit between neighbours, in goods they could see and hold. The economy did not grind to a stop. It changed tokens.

The explanation offered two states: believe, or fall into chaos. History offers a third, and it is the ordinary one. When a fiction fails, people swap things.

## III. A trick is still a trick

The usual defence is scale. The state is the largest employer and the largest buyer; millions depend on the loop continuing. But a con with a million dependents is still a con. The number of people who need the trick to keep running says nothing about whether it is one.

Nor is control an accusation. It is an observable outcome. Look at what the system does, and control is what it produces.

What the explanation got wrong is the word "designed", and later "engineered". Both need a designer, an engineer. None is required. Control can fall out of the system the way exhaust falls out of an engine, without anyone deciding it should. Keep the outcome. Drop the author.

## IV. Starving is a myth

There is always an objection waiting at this point. You cannot leave. You still have to eat, pay tax, hold some token. Refuse the fiction and you starve.

Nobody starves from seeing. The objection imagines an exit no one attempts, then uses its impossibility to close every other door.

And notice whose interest the objection serves. Framing the Trick as something you do not get to leave leaves room for the Trick to stay. Every wise voice that says "of course you can't get out, but you can be aware" gives it tenure. The Trick does not need you to believe it is real. It needs you to believe it is permanent.

## V. Fiction, illusion, lie

The explanation used three words as if they were one. A fiction can be known as fiction by everyone and still work, like the rules of a game. An illusion is something mistaken for real. A lie needs a liar.

Most people handing over a banknote are not deceived. They know, more or less, what it is. They hand it over anyway, because everyone else does. The system never checks what you believe. It checks what you do. You can pay the tax, obey the rule, spend the token, and be privately certain the whole thing is a con. The machine cannot tell the difference.

So belief is individual. It can change in an instant, alone, with no one's permission and no one's help. What holds the fiction up is not belief at all. It is performance: people acting as if, whatever they think.

## VI. A face in the clouds

The natural next step is to say the performance is collective. It is not. Collective behaviour is a face in the clouds. Humans are wired to see patterns, and millions of separate acts look, from far enough away, like a crowd moving with one will. There is no crowd. There is no "the market" that wants, no "the public" that thinks, no "society" that believes. Those are faces we draw on vapour.

Change is the same. Zoom in and you see individuals. Zoom out and you see a pattern. Zoom into the pattern and it is individuals again. Like a fractal drawing, it has no floor and no privileged scale. What we call change is mostly reframing: the same things, seen at a different resolution.

Beware the collective. Use the word to point if you must. The moment you believe the face is looking back at you, you have joined the Trick.

## VII. The Trick deludes itself

If there is no designer and no crowd, what is left? The Trick deludes itself, because that is its only option.

It cannot choose its strategy. It has no one to do the choosing. It persists only as long as people keep acting as if it is real, and it exists only in that acting. So it has one move: go on deluding, or dissolve. It takes the move, and keeps taking it. Whether that looks coordinated or emergent is a distraction. It was never choosing.

The "self" it deludes is nobody in particular. It is just us, one at a time, mistaking our own performance for something outside us.

## VIII. The walls

A face in the clouds leaves nothing behind. The Trick does. It is best looked at like *The Truman Show*: both there and not there. The sky is painted. The walls are real.

Who built them? In the film, not the one who believes. Truman is the believer. The walls are built and kept up by everyone around him, the actors and the crew, who know exactly what it is and perform it anyway. That is the uncomfortable part. You do not need to believe in the Trick to build its walls. You only need to keep performing it. Seeing through it while staying on script changes nothing.

And what does recognition do? Truman sees the seams, walks to the edge of the set, and leaves through the door. The show goes on for its audience. Recognition is not a process - it has no steps and needs no one else. It is the end of being tricked, for one person, now.

The Trick remains until the last person stops playing their part.

## IX. Nothing. Really. Matters.

The explanation began by calling the fiction necessary. Necessary for what? For millions of strangers to act as one. Take away the need for that, and the necessity goes with it.

What is left is not a reward. The one who sees gets nothing for seeing: no escape for anyone else, no wall brought down in their lifetime, no place among the few who knew. Nothing.

And the walls themselves are made of nothing. No designer, no crowd, no substance, only performance hardened into concrete. Nothing, made solid. Nothing, really, mattering.

Read back through this and notice where you nodded. The banknote you handed over, knowing what it was. The shrug that says you can't leave. The market that wants, the public that thinks, the face in the clouds you half believed was watching you. The walls you have mortared yourself, on script, while privately certain it was all a con. None of that makes you a fool. It makes you one of the cast.

No one will tell you which part you are playing, and no one is keeping count. The door is where it was in the film, at the edge of the painted sky. Recognising it costs nothing, and it pays nothing.

So, just between you and this page: do you feel tricked? It's okay.

Nothing. Really. Matters.
