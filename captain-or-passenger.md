# Captain or Passenger: Repositioning Bitcoin

Carl · 24 November 2026

**Barter and gift. Local footpath. You walk.**

**Banks. Railway. You're a passenger with a ticket.**

**Bitcoin. Open sea. You're the captain of your own boat.**

## Starting point

Bitcoin matters for what it does, not what it is. Most talk about it is about price. This document leaves price aside and looks at what Bitcoin lets people do, using a different language so the reader can form a different relationship with it.

## Three ways to move value

The same human activities can run on three different systems: exchanging, saving, gifting, paying for work, supporting someone far away. The activity stays the same. What changes is the route, and who controls it.

|  | Barter and gift | Banks | Bitcoin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Image | Local footpath | Railway | Open sea |
| Your role | You walk | Passenger with a ticket | Captain of your own boat |
| Who owns the route | No one | A company | No one |
| Reach | As far as you can walk | Only where the track goes | Global |
| What it rests on | Relationships, being face to face | The banking network | Verification by anyone |
| Ledger | None | Private, held by the bank | Public, held by everyone |

Barter and gift are the original, the human default. Banks added reach by taking away the freedoms. Bitcoin keeps the reach and gives the freedoms back: it is the freedoms of barter and gift, extended to strangers at a distance.

The differences from barter are real. Bitcoin replaces trust in a relationship with verification, which is what lets it work between strangers. It removes the need for each side to want what the other has. And its ledger is public, so on visibility barter remains the freest of the three.

Bitcoin isn't a new kind of money in this framing. It's the open sea: no one owns it, anyone can sail it, and control only reappears at the harbours, the on- and off-ramps where it meets the railway.

## The four guarantees

Four promises describe the open sea. Each is built from properties of Bitcoin that don't change, and each is something the reader can test for themselves.

**Open to anyone.**

**You have what you hold.**

**No one to ask.**

**No one to stop you.**

They read as two pairs: access and ownership, then permission and interference.

- **Open to anyone.** No bank account, ID, credit history, minimum amount or nationality required. This comes first because, for most people, the first problem isn't censorship but exclusion.
- **You have what you hold.** Whoever holds the keys owns the coins, with no issuer and no account. It also carries an unspoken warning: if you don't hold it, you don't have it. Holding isn't a separate function, just what you have between journeys.
- **No one to ask.** This line has three meanings. No one to ask permission. No one in charge, so no CEO to pressure, buy or replace. And no one to complain to, so no help desk, no appeals and no refunds.
- **No one to stop you.** No authority can block a valid transfer or seize coins without the keys. This is true only because there is no one to lean on.

### The mirror

Read in reverse, the four lines describe the railway exactly. The reader can check each one against their bank account today.

| The open sea | The railway |
| --- | --- |
| Open to anyone. | Closed to some. |
| You have what you hold. | They hold what you have. |
| No one to ask. | Someone to ask. |
| No one to stop you. | Someone to stop you. |

Barter and gift pass all four tests as well. Banks are the only one of the three that fail them.

The same inversion applies to the systems themselves. On the railway, the system is opaque and the people are transparent: you can't audit the bank, but the bank sees all of you. On the open sea, the system is transparent and the people are pseudonymous: anyone can audit the sea, and the boats carry no names.

## Captain or passenger

The whole repositioning reduces to one question for the reader: **are you the captain, or a passenger?**

It needs no explanation and no Bitcoin vocabulary, and everyone knows which they would rather be. Most people realise they have been passengers their whole financial life. That realisation is the first step.

- **The passenger** holds a ticket. Someone else owns the track, sets the timetable and decides who boards.
- **The captain** has their own boat, sets their own course and keeps to no one's timetable.

### Ferries on the open sea

There are ferries and cruise liners on the open sea too: exchanges, ETFs and custodial apps. It's the same sea, but you are a passenger again, holding a ticket while someone else is at the helm.

So the question is never "do you use Bitcoin?" It is "are you sailing your own boat, or are you on a ferry?" That keeps the distinction about the relationship, not the technology.

### Seamanship

A captain needs seamanship: self-custody, keys and backups. Framed this way, learning is competence rather than a burden, because nobody would put to sea without learning to sail. Freedom and responsibility arrive together, and the four lines say so without a single warning label.

In practice, seamanship is a handful of habits:

- Keep two backups of your keys, in two places, neither of them online.
- Send a small amount first, then the rest.
- Check the address before you send.
- Never type your recovery words into a website or app, and never share them.
- Keep a small wallet for spending and a separate one for savings.
- Use a new address each time you receive.

### Reachability

Bitcoin excludes no one, but in practice people need a mobile device, some connectivity and a way to get their first sats. Bitcoin makes the guarantees true; the community makes them reachable through P2P trades, vouchers, local loops and physical bearer objects. The harbour is the real battleground: getting someone onto a boat matters more than getting them to agree.

### Agreeing isn't sailing

The biggest risk isn't that the reader disagrees. It's that they agree and stay a passenger. Thinking Bitcoin is interesting and holding your own keys are a long way apart. So the text should keep pointing at the reader's own ticket: whose name is on it, and who can stop the train?

## Powered by Bitcoin

Bitcoin should sit under the bonnet, as a white-label layer. Users see the guarantees, not the technology.

There are good precedents. Nobody says they are using SMTP: they send an email. "Intel Inside" didn't hide the chip: it put a sticker on the lid that vouched for the whole machine. Keeping Bitcoin out of view sidesteps what puts people off: price talk, ideology, scams and jargon.

Keeping the Bitcoin name out also matters for de-conditioning. Most readers have already made up their minds about Bitcoin, and saying the word triggers that opinion before the next sentence lands. Lines that describe only freedoms get past the reflex. The reader agrees before knowing what they're agreeing to.

### The risk

White-labelling has mostly been done in the wrong direction. Apps that use Lightning under the hood usually hold the user's balance for them. The plumbing is permissionless, but the experience is fully governable. When Bitcoin disappears from view, the keys tend to go with it.

So brand the property and hide the machinery: the four lines are the sticker on the lid.

## Principles for the language

The aim is radical: a first step in un-conditioning the reader. Conditioning here isn't a false belief someone was given. It is things that have stopped looking strange, like needing permission to use your own money. You can't argue people out of what they don't see. You can only make it visible.

1. **What it does, not what it is.** Describe Bitcoin as something you use, not an asset you own and watch.
2. **Lead without the name.** Let the freedoms land first, so the reader's existing opinion of Bitcoin doesn't fire.
3. **Use contrast, not argument.** The mirror lines do the work, and the reader reaches the conclusion in their own head.
4. **Make observations, not doctrine.** Every line must be something the reader can test. Slogans to repeat would just be another form of conditioning.
5. **Choose infrastructure, not sides.** Footpath, railway and sea are routes, not ideologies, and people can use all three, journey by journey.
6. **Put the human activity first.** Exchange came before either system. Both are optional infrastructure for something people already do.
7. **State freedom and responsibility together.** The double meanings carry the hard edge without warning labels.

**Are you the captain, or a passenger?**
