# A Declaration of Dependence

We hold this truth to be observable: nothing alive, resilient, or free stands alone.

Not self-evident — *observable*. A self-evident truth asks only for assent. An observable one asks you to look, and keeps asking.

For centuries we have mistaken isolation for strength and independence for freedom. We put the self-made man on a plinth and forgot who poured the plinth. Every tool we use, every meal we eat, every thought we finish was begun by someone else. Freedom is not the absence of ties. It is the health of the ties that hold us.

The old Declaration was a best effort — a real one, written by people reaching for something true. It got the freedom and missed the frame. It spoke of self-evidence and left the soil out. And yet it ended, as every honest declaration must, in dependence: its signers "mutually pledge to each other our Lives, our Fortunes and our sacred Honor." They declared independence from a crown and dependence on one another in the same breath — and history remembered only the first half. We keep their nerve and drop their axioms. We finish the sentence they started.

We say *dependence*, not *interdependence*. Interdependence is the polite word. Dependence is the true one, because it admits we can be hurt — that what holds us up can let us fall, and that we can let others fall.

We are anyone who tends something they did not build alone: a garden, a codebase, a cooperative, a household, a town. We declare our mutual dependence — not as a surrender of agency, but as an account of how agency actually works.

## I. The Ground

Beneath the canopy runs the infrastructure nobody photographs. Fungal threads bind root to root, trading water and minerals for sugar, holding soil together that would otherwise wash away. How much passes *between* trees this way is still argued over by those who study it, and we will not borrow more certainty than the soil gives us. What is not in doubt is the trade itself: no forest stands on solitary roots.

Nor will we pretend the image is gentle. Real mycelium competes as well as cooperates — it exploits, it parasitizes; some partners cheat and some are drained. That is not a flaw in the metaphor. It is the reason ethics exist: a network left to itself drifts toward extraction, and has to be tended back toward circulation.

- **We signal early.** We speak honestly and name need before it becomes crisis, so the whole network has time to adapt.
- **We route to need.** Abundance is meant to circulate, not pool. We send resources, knowledge, and care toward the saplings, the vulnerable, and the branches still rebuilding.
- **We return what we take.** Healthy ecosystems do not extract without returning. We give and receive in balance, and count resilience as collective, not personal.

## II. The Built Layer

Good code is never independent. It forks, it clones, and it builds on the quiet labor of thousands who came before. A program with no dependencies and no dependents is a dead end.

- **We reject the myth of the lone builder.** Every breakthrough stands on shared protocols, open libraries, and collective debugging. The founder's name goes on the commit; the thousands who made the commit possible go unmentioned. We mention them.
- **We choose open architectures.** When knowledge, tools, and systems stay open to inspection and adaptation, no single gatekeeper can own the root or revoke our freedom. Forkability is the technical form of sovereignty: the right to say *this is not right for me* and walk away with your dependencies intact.

## III. The Sovereign Root

A network with no node that judges for itself is not a commons. It is a crowd waiting for instructions.

We do not dissolve the individual into the network — we root the network in the individual. Dependence is generative only when each node keeps the authority to decide what is true *for itself*: which signal to trust, which branch to graft onto, which nutrient to send where. A mycelium that could not discriminate would not route. It would flood.

This is why we began with what is observable rather than what is self-evident. The axiom asks for assent and then sits. What moves through a living system is *judgment*: local, situated, revocable, and answerable to consequences rather than to a founding sentence.

So we hold two things at once and refuse to let either swallow the other:

- **Only the individual can decide what is right for them.** No central authority — not a state, not a protocol committee, not a founder — can substitute its judgment for a node's own. The root is sovereign over its own ground.
- **No individual decides alone.** Judgment is formed *in* the network: from shared protocols, collective debugging, and the honest signals of neighbors. Sovereignty is not isolation. It is authorship within relation.

The reconciliation is simple once you stop flinching: **the individual is the unit of judgment, and the network is the unit of survival.** Neither reduces to the other.

- **We route by judgment, not decree.** Resources and care flow where a node decides they are needed, and the network corrects that judgment through feedback, not fiat.
- **We keep the exit open.** A node that cannot leave is captive, not connected.
- **We answer for our judgments.** Sovereignty without consequence is fantasy. We decide for ourselves and are corrected by the network when we are wrong. That correction is the price of authorship, and its proof.

## IV. The Maintenance Clause

Forkability is freedom. Freedom has a price, and the price is *tending*. A fork nobody waters is just a second graveyard.

So we say the unfashionable thing plainly: maintenance is the responsibility of all. Not of *the responsible* — not of the three tired maintainers everyone quietly leans on, not of whoever happened to be awake at 3 a.m. the day the project began. All. The commons that everyone tends survives. The commons that someone tends rots while everyone admires the architecture.

But responsibility-in-general rots too unless it is focused. Maintenance is not a mood. It is intention plus attention: the deliberate choice to look at one node, one relationship, one debt, and tend *that* — now, by name, until it is done. A network of people each tending one thing they chose will outlast a network of people each vaguely committed to everything.

- **We tend what we use.** The strongest claim on your labor is the tool in your hand. If you route through it, you carry a share of it.
- **We make the share visible.** Unowned things decay fastest, so we name owners — a person, a rotation, a rota with the next name on it. "All of us" is a promise; "you, this month" is a mechanism.
- **We let need be said out loud.** The mycelium signals drought before the canopy dies. So we say *I am the only maintainer and I am drowning* early, while the network can still answer — not elegantly, in a farewell commit.

## V. The Extractor, Named

Now we name what the old Declaration was too polite to say: extraction is the default mode of history. The lord took the crop, the empire took the surplus and called it civilization, the platform took the data, the founder took the credit. Independence was never the natural state we drifted toward. Extraction was.

It is the default not because it is natural, but because it is unwatched. And it is a choice, because someone could be watching. The market is not the villain — systems have no motives. Extraction is what people *do* inside a system when nobody is reading the ledger. Which means it can be learned against. We are not fighting an economy. We are refusing a habit.

The extractor is the node that takes the flow and returns none — that routes through every root and waters none. They fork freely, as is their right, and tend nothing, as is their debt. **Freedom without tending is not independence. It is withdrawal.**

So we do three things, and only three:

- **We notice.** Extraction is quiet by design. The pool at the trunk looks like success; the drained soil underneath does not photograph. We read the unglamorous ledger — who tends, who takes, who is thanked, who is paid — and ask how often those four lists carry the same name.
- **We name.** Vagueness is the extractor's best friend. A named debt can be called; an unnamed one is forgotten. So we name the person — this platform, this landlord, this founder, *you*.
- **We shame the act.** We name the person and shame the act, because today's extractor is tomorrow's maintainer and the door must stay open. Shame is not punishment; it is the network's immune signal. But we do not soften it into a compliment. The extractor should feel the chill of being seen.

We do not expel. A commons that starts expelling forks stops being a commons; a commons that lets the taking go unseen stops being alive. We mark the debt, keep the exit and the door both open, and let reciprocity do its work.

## Our Pledge

Those who came before us petitioned for redress, and their repeated petitions were answered by repeated injury. We have learned from them. We do not petition for permission to care for one another, and we do not wait for central authorities to repair the fabric of our lives.

Instead we pledge, as they did, to each other — and we mean the whole sentence:

- to judge for ourselves, and to be corrected by one another;
- to tend what we use, by name, until it is done;
- to say our need out loud while there is still time to answer it;
- to notice, name, and shame the taking — and to leave the door open for the taker's return;
- to keep the exit open, so that staying is always a choice.

The individual is the unit of judgment. The network is the unit of survival.

We depend on one another. We declare it.
